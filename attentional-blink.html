What's Actually Happening

The Phenomenon

When you pick out one target from a rapid stream, you become blind to a second target that arrives roughly 200 to 500 milliseconds later. Raymond, Shapiro and Arnell named this the attentional blink in 1992. The second digit is on the screen for the same 100 ms as every other item, and your eyes are pointed right at it. Yet on many trials it leaves no trace in your experience.

There is a strange exception. A second digit that comes immediately after the first (a gap of 100 ms) is often seen just fine. This is called lag-1 sparing. The blink only opens once the first target has started to be processed.

Why It Happens

A popular account, from Chun and Potter, splits perception into two stages. In the first, every item in the stream is identified quickly and in parallel, but these identifications fade within a fraction of a second. In the second, a slow, one-at-a-time process consolidates an item into a form you can remember and report. While that stage is busy with the first digit, the second one is identified and then lost before its turn comes.

The missed digit is not simply unseen. In a 1996 study, Luck, Vogel and Shapiro showed people words during the blink. Their brains produced the N400, a response to word meaning, even for words they could not report. Sergent, Baillet and Dehaene found that brain activity for seen and missed targets matches for the first few hundred milliseconds. It diverges only when a later, brain-wide wave either fires or fails to.

The Philosophical Point

The blink separates two things we tend to lump together: a stimulus being processed and a stimulus being experienced. Your brain read the missed digit, and even extracted its meaning. What it never did was make that content available to the rest of you: to memory, to report, to the part of you that clicks the buttons.

Dennett calls consciousness fame in the brain. Contents compete for influence, and the ones that win get to shape what you say and do next. On this view, the missed digit was not hidden behind a curtain. It lost the competition. The first digit was still holding the stage, and the second one's moment passed.

It also raises an awkward question. When you missed a digit, did the stream feel like it had a gap? For most people it doesn't. You don't experience the blink. You only learn about it from the score.

Tips

  • Keep your eyes on the center of the box for the whole stream
  • Report digits in any order. Only which digits you saw counts.
  • Guess if you had a hunch. Chance with eight possible digits is low, so lucky guesses barely move the results.
  • Close other tabs if the page warns about stutter. Timing matters here.
  • Some studies find the blink shrinks when people attend less intensely, for example while listening to music
